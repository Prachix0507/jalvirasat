    03 / Ways of holding water

    Different places,
    different systems.

    A concise typology: local names cover many designs, and real structures can combine functions. The comparison describes broad patterns, not a rule for every site.

    SystemHow it captures & stores waterTypical historical spanTypical region
    Stepwell
    baoli · vav    		Steps descend to a well or reservoir so people can reach a changing groundwater level; masonry may also retain and channel water.Prominent medieval examples, with regional forms built over many centuries.Arid and semi-arid western and northern India, alongside other local traditions.
    JohadA small earthen or stone embankment slows seasonal runoff, stores water in a pond and can support groundwater recharge.Long-standing community practice; dates depend on each structure and revival history.Especially associated with Rajasthan and other dryland landscapes.
    Tank
    eri · kere    		A bund or embankment holds surface runoff; channels, sluices and linked basins distribute water, often for irrigation.Local systems span centuries; individual tanks may be expanded and repaired in different eras.Regional forms across India, including Tamil Nadu eris and Karnataka keres.
    Kund
    kund · kuṇḍ    		A prepared catchment directs rainwater into a deep, often covered cistern that limits evaporation and keeps stored water accessible.Varies widely; many examples reflect long adaptation to arid conditions.Dry western India, including Rajasthan's desert districts.

    “Typical” is comparative, not universal. Zing and surangam have distinct forms and are described individually in their field notes.

    04 / About the archive

    Remembering how we learned to live with water.

    Jal Virasat—“water heritage”—is a small digital field museum for indigenous water-management knowledge. It brings stepwells, johads, tanks, kunds and other locally shaped systems into one browsable archive, pairing each record with its mechanics, place and source trail.

    These systems are not interchangeable relics or ready-made fixes. They document ways communities read rainfall, terrain and geology, shared maintenance, and adapted storage to local seasons. In a warming climate and a period of mounting water stress, that situated knowledge can broaden conservation conversations—alongside present-day science, infrastructure and community priorities.
